- **Parks Canada (National Parks)** publishes the [reservation launch dates](https://parks.canada.ca/voyage-travel/reserve) in November or December for the following summer. Reservations launch on different dates for each park, usually spread between mid-January and mid-February.
  - [Reservation Website](https://reservation.pc.gc.ca/)
- **Alberta Parks (Provincial Parks)** uses a [rolling-window system](https://albertaparks.ca/albertaparksca/visit-our-parks/camping-in-alberta-parks/online-reservations/): reservations open 90 days in advance for individual and backcountry camping, and 180 days in advance for group and comfort camping.
  - [Reservation Website](https://albertaparks.ca/reserve/)
- **BC Parks** takes reservations through [camping.bcparks.ca](https://camping.bcparks.ca/) on a rolling window — check the site for the current booking window.
- **US National Park Service (NPS)** — in particular [Montana's Glacier National Park](https://www.nps.gov/glac/planyourvisit/reservation-campgrounds.htm) just south of the border — uses a rolling window with reservations up to 6 months in advance.
  - [Reservation Website](https://www.recreation.gov/)

**🔔 Sold out? Get alerted about cancellations.** These services watch the reservation systems and notify you when a spot frees up — you still book it yourself through the official site:
- [Schnerp](https://www.schnerp.com/): Monitors campground reservation sites for cancellations. Comes with 10 free alerts, with paid plans for more.
- [Scree Alerts](https://screealerts.com/): Watches high-demand backcountry campsites across Parks Canada and BC Parks (currently in beta).

## Camping on Alberta public land

Outside the parks, much of the Eastern Slopes is ordinary public land, where **random
camping** — pitching where you like, no booking, no facilities — is legal rather than
tolerated. It is the usual answer in the Ghost, the Bighorn, and the Livingstone, and it is
how most people sleep near Abraham Lake. The rules are province-wide and worth knowing
before you rely on it:

- **A [Public Lands Camping Pass](https://www.alberta.ca/public-lands-camping-pass) is required** for everyone 18 and older, year-round, anywhere in the pass area — which runs the length of the Eastern Slopes from Grande Prairie to Waterton. It costs $30 a year or $20 for three days, plus a one-time $8 Wildlife Identification Number if you have never bought an Alberta licence. It is not a reservation and does not hold you a spot.
- **First come, first served, and 14 days maximum.** After 14 days the unit has to move at least 1 km and stay moved for 72 hours.
- **Random camping is not permitted within 1 km of a Public Land Recreation Area, a provincial park, or a Provincial Recreation Area**, which rules out the ground immediately around the serviced campgrounds.
- **In the four Kananaskis-area zones it is also not permitted within 1 km of a road** — Kananaskis, McLean Creek, Sibbald, and Cataract Creek. In practice that means carrying your camp a kilometre off the highway, which is why this region is the one where the answer is usually a booked site.
- **In the Bighorn and Front Range zones, campsites must be 100 m back from any lakeshore.** This is the rule people are most often unaware of, and it does not sit comfortably with the lakeside spots those areas are known for — check the map for whether you are inside a zone before assuming the shoreline is fair game.
- **Some zones allow random camping only in designated *camping nodes*** — marked sites with an outhouse, a fire ring, and sometimes a bin. They are free, still first-come, and still need the pass. Follow the signs; where nodes exist, camping between them is not permitted.
- **Check for fire bans and closures before lighting anything** — [Alberta fire bans](https://www.albertafirebans.ca/) and [public land closures](https://www.alberta.ca/public-land-closures).

The authoritative source for what is allowed where is the province's
[Interactive Recreation Map](https://esrd.maps.arcgis.com/apps/webappviewer/index.html?id=e5651574de1342d7ae8277ef415864be),
which draws the pass boundary, the [public land use zones](https://www.alberta.ca/public-land-use-zones),
and the recreation areas. The rules themselves are on
[Camping on public land](https://www.alberta.ca/camping-on-public-land).

The region pages name the zones and the established spots each area is known for.

## Camping on BC Crown land

West of the divide the system is different enough to catch out anyone arriving with Alberta
habits. There is **no pass and no boundary to be inside of**, and the province runs a network
of actual campgrounds on Crown land rather than leaving you to find your own ground.

**Recreation sites** are the main event: around 1,200 of them have camping, run by
[Recreation Sites and Trails BC](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/sports-culture/recreation/camping-hiking/sites-trails),
mostly with a fire ring, a picnic table and a pit toilet. **Most are free** — across the
regions listed here it is about four in five — and the rest charge $15 to $25 a night,
collected **in cash** by a site operator. Almost all are first come, first served; a small
number of fee sites take reservations, and the site's own listing is the only reliable word on
which. Use the [site and trail search](https://www.sitesandtrailsbc.ca/search) to check status,
fees and closures before going. Each BC region page links the ones worth knowing.

Use is governed by the
[Forest Recreation Regulation](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/lc/statreg/16_2004),
and the [rules](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/sports-culture/recreation/camping-hiking/sites-trails/planning/rules)
are short: **14 consecutive days maximum**, no long-term structures, pack out what you pack
in, quiet from 11 pm to 7 am, and campfires kept to 0.5 m × 0.5 m inside the ring provided.

**Camping elsewhere on Crown land** is allowed too, and free. The province's
[Crown land permission policy](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/farming-natural-resources-and-industry/natural-resource-use/land-water-use/crown-land/permissions.pdf)
(PDF, section 8.2) puts it plainly: *"Any person may camp on Crown land for up to 14
consecutive calendar days"*, with the count resetting only after you and your vehicle have
been off the site for 72 hours — the same shape as the Alberta rule, without the pass. What
matters is section 7.1, which says where the permission **does not** apply: parks,
conservancies and ecological reserves, wildlife management areas, municipalities, land under
lease, federal land, and any Crown land *"posted or otherwise signed to prohibit"* it. A gate
or a sign is the whole answer, so read them.

**Getting there is the part that bites.** Recreation sites are reached by
[Forest Service roads](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/sports-culture/recreation/camping-hiking/sites-trails/planning/access),
shared with loaded logging trucks that cannot stop for you. Many are radio-assist with posted
channels; yield to industrial traffic whether or not you have a radio, and expect conditions
to change without a sign. Check
[fire prohibitions and restrictions](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/safety/wildfire-status/prevention/fire-bans-and-restrictions)
before lighting anything.
